WaterLink

A broken water point looks like a working one until you get there.

Most rural families fetch water at a hand pump or a protected well. When it breaks, the news travels slowly: the walk is wasted, the repair report disappears, and nobody is clearly responsible.

  • Up to one in three hand pumps does not work at any one time across Africa.

    UNICEF Office of Innovation, “Smart Pumps”

  • Even with a repair contract, people can be without clean water for at least 2–3 days.

    UNICEF, “Smart Pumps”: repairs are due 48–72 hours after a sensor reports a failure

  • In Kyuso, Kenya, the cost of repairs (19%), the time they take (17%) and how often pumps break (17%) top the list of complaints about hand pumps.

    UNICEF, citing a user survey (Oxford/RFL, 2014)

39,959 survey records for Kenya in the Water Point Data Exchange.

The last confirmed state before the walk, and a repair that is someone’s job

  1. A public board.Each water point shows its state, who confirmed it and for how long it holds. After 12 hours it says “unclear”, never an old green. It works on a screen at the shop, on a phone, and as a printed sheet.
  2. A repair that counts.An outage report becomes a job with a named person and visible blockers (parts, money, staff). “Repaired” is not “working”: only a test on site turns the point green.
  3. A reliable alternative.If a point is down, the board names the nearest one, but only if it was freshly confirmed and its route and access were checked.

The same board in Kiswahili (draft translation, labelled on the board), with the QR code to the SMS view. Also available as phone view and printed sheet.

A working prototype with the important features, live today

  • Public board and household view: state, time of the check, validity; English and Kiswahili (draft); printable sheet.
  • Report → job → progress and blockers → repair → test on site, with history; roles enforced on the server.
  • Uncertainty is shown, not hidden: stale, conflicting, offline and sensor-hint states never look working.
  • Alternatives only when verified: route and access checks, opening hours, adding a water point by a tap on the map.
  • SMS simulation and a replay of real 2014 smart-handpump data (Kwale) as a sensor hint. Practice mode for anyone, no login.
  • Checked: 85 unit tests and 37 browser tests (repair flow, offline, accessibility, phone width, print) on every push.

What is real, what is simulated, what is not connected

Real

  • 42 places in Marachi East, Busia, from 60 survey records of the Water Point Data Exchange (CC BY 4.0); repeated surveys of one place are merged.
  • Roads and places from OpenStreetMap.
  • Weekly pump volumes from Kwale, Kenya, 2014 (openwashdata, CC BY 4.0).
  • All rules, the server and the tests.

Simulated, and labelled so

  • People: the operator, the technician and coordination.
  • Confirmations, reports and repairs.
  • Walking routes and opening hours.
  • The state of the places: survey status is historical and never shown as current.

Not connected yet

  • An SMS gateway for real messages.
  • Accounts per water point (today: one shared demo code).
  • Live sensors, payments, an AI service.
  • A pilot partner. We make no impact claims.

“Working” means water can be drawn. It says nothing about drinking-water quality, and every board says so.

What we have not proven, and how we will measure it

Fresh confirmations
Share of water points with a confirmation younger than its validity. Tells us whether operators really confirm.
Time to a repair that counts
Median time from report to claim, and to the test on site. Shows where the days go: information, money, parts or people.
Wasted walks
Households that walked to a broken point, before and after the board is in place.
Understanding
Does anyone read “Working” as “clean water”? The answer must be no.

The plan

One neighbourhood, a handful of water points, one board, confirmations by a named person per point.

A partner who is responsible for the points: a county water office, an NGO, or a maintenance service of the FundiFix kind.

Baseline first. We measure the four numbers before the board goes up and agree the targets afterwards, not before.

We do not claim an impact today. We have a prototype, a data analysis and a plan to find out.

What we learned while building it

  1. An old green is worse than no answer.So every confirmation expires, and an expired point reads “unclear”.
  2. Repaired is not working.Only a test on site, by the operator, turns a point green again.
  3. “Working” is not “safe”.The board says so every time; we make no water-quality claim.
  4. A dot on the map is not a recommendation.An alternative needs a fresh confirmation and a checked route first.
  5. Records are not places.Checking the data showed that 60 survey records are 42 places. We merged them and said so.
  6. Say what is unchecked.The Kiswahili texts are a draft, compared with Swahili sources, and labelled as a draft on the board.
  7. Test where it hurts.A test run against the live site found a clock bug (a device 9 seconds behind the server) that looking at the screen never showed.
  8. Do not invent evidence.No testimonials, no impact figures. What is open is on the page “Numbers behind our assumptions”.

Next: one neighbourhood, one partner, four numbers

  1. Find the partner and the place.A county water office, NGO or maintenance service, and a neighbourhood where the board can hang and someone passes it on the way to water.
  2. Connect what is simulated.A real SMS gateway, an account for every water point and operator, backups and data protection for Kenya.
  3. Measure.Baseline, then the four numbers on the previous page, with the people who use it.
  4. Decide with evidence.Scale up, change or stop, depending on what the numbers say.
